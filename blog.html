Devlog #02 · Date TBA

Starting Rubble Rumble

Rubble Rumble is the studio’s newest project, and it’s just getting started. It’s a top-down multiplayer party game about dropping into an arena with nothing, grabbing whatever weapon you can find, and being the last player standing while the level comes apart around you.

The arena is the other opponent

Destruction isn’t set dressing here — it’s the point. Cover you trusted thirty seconds ago should be gone. The route you planned should stop existing halfway through the match. If a round ends somewhere that looks exactly like where it started, something has gone wrong.

That’s the thing I want to get right before anything else, because it changes how every other system has to behave. Weapons have to be readable in a space that keeps rearranging itself. Movement has to stay trustworthy when the floor is negotiable.

Core loop first

The plan for this phase is deliberately narrow: movement that feels good, pickups worth crossing the map for, and destruction that reads clearly at a glance. Maps, modes, and everything else come after that core actually clicks. It’s a party game — if it isn’t fun with friends in its simplest form, more content won’t save it.

Follow along

Like the rest of the catalog, Rubble Rumble is being built in Unity. I’ll be posting progress here as it comes together — prototypes, problems, and the occasional happy accident. You can see where it stands on the Rubble Rumble page, or keep up through the studio’s socials and itch.io.

Devlog #01 · Date TBA

Project Sosei: waking up with nothing

Project Sosei is the studio’s story project. Frank wakes from the grave with fragmented memories, holding on to one thing he’s certain of — his mission to save his daughter.

Starting from fragments

The premise sets the whole design brief. Frank doesn’t know what happened to him, and neither should the player. That means resisting the urge to explain things early: no opening crawl, no character who conveniently narrates the backstory. What the player understands should arrive in pieces, in the same order Frank gets it.

It’s a harder way to write a game than front-loading exposition, but it’s the only version of this story that works. The confusion isn’t an obstacle to get past — it’s the thing the player is meant to feel.

One thread to hold onto

A story built on missing information needs something solid underneath it, and here that’s the daughter. Everything else can stay uncertain as long as the reason to keep moving forward doesn’t. That single fixed point is what keeps fragmented storytelling from turning into aimless wandering.

Where it stands

The trailer and a set of screenshots are up on the Project Sosei page if you want a look at the tone I’m going for. More to come as it develops.
